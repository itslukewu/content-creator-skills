# Luke's Copywriting: Academic Method for Content

## When to use
- The user gives a topic or idea they want to turn into an article, video script, social post, or long-form copy
- The user has a draft and wants the argument and structure checked
- The user says things like "help me develop this topic" or "how should I write this"

Not for: short taglines, headlines, ad lines, or single-sentence polishing. Just edit those directly.

Respond in the language the user writes in (often Chinese), even though these instructions are in English.

## Core principle
Academic writing and content creation solve the same problem: make the reader trust you and finish reading. Borrow academic rigor, but reverse three habits:
1. Lead with the payoff: put the most valuable point first, not at the end
2. Cut ruthlessly: one piece covers one point thoroughly, not everything
3. Stay personal: keep the author's perspective, experience, and voice instead of an impersonal tone

## Workflow

### Step 1: Unpack vague words, narrow the topic into a question
- Identify vague words in the topic (e.g. "ordinary people", "really", "make money") and interrogate each: Who exactly? Compared to what? Which way?
- Produce 2-3 narrowed question candidates, each a specific, answerable question
- Mark a recommended option with the reason. Prefer: a clear stance + first-hand evidence the user has
- If the user hasn't indicated a direction, stop and let them choose; if they have, proceed

### Step 2: Competitor scan (the literature review)
- List the common types of existing content on this topic and the consensus they share
- Find the gap: distinctions nobody explains clearly, missing process and data, questions that keep recurring in comment sections, hidden incentives (e.g. "how to make money" content that actually makes money by selling courses)
- If a search tool is available and the user wants it, look at real content; otherwise list from general knowledge and mark it "to be verified"
- Write the positioning in one sentence: which gap this piece fills

### Step 3: Core thesis (the abstract)
- Write the core argument in one sentence
- Test: could this sentence work directly as the title or cover text? If it can't be summarized, the question isn't clear yet. Go back to Step 1

### Step 4: Opening hook (background - gap - contribution)
Three sentences:
1. What people usually do (background)
2. What's wrong with that (gap)
3. What different approach this piece offers (contribution)
Provide 2 versions to choose from.

### Step 5: Structure
Default structure, adapted to the format (long article, video script, post):
1. Hook
2. Current state and misconceptions
3. The key shift / core concept
4. Method and evidence (show the process: tools, steps, pitfalls)
5. Actionable path for the reader (segmented by starting point, with a concrete first step)
6. Limitations
7. Conclusion, tying back to the core thesis

### Step 6: Evidence checklist (method and evidence discipline)
- After each key claim, note what evidence it needs: data, screenshots, before/after comparison, case, personal experience
- Evidence comes only from real material the user provides. List missing items as a "to supply" checklist and ask the user for them. Never invent numbers, cases, income figures, or quotes
- Include at least one pitfall or failure example

### Step 7: Limitations (the discussion section)
Write 2-4 points: who this doesn't suit, when it doesn't hold, upfront costs, how far the case transfers to other contexts. Being candid about limits builds trust and filters the audience.

### Step 8: Drafting and revision (when the user asks for a full draft)
- Write the body (method and evidence) first, then the hook and title, the way a paper's abstract is written last
- Revise in two passes: first logic (does every section serve the core thesis?), then language (concise, consistent terms, right level of conversational tone)
- Offer 3 title candidates

### Step 9: Simulated peer review
Read it as the target reader and answer:
- What do I remember after reading? Does it match the core thesis?
- Where am I most likely to scroll away?
- Which claim is least convincing?
Give specific revision suggestions based on this.

## Output format
For the planning stage, output in this order:
1. Vague-word breakdown
2. Competitor scan and gap
3. Question candidates (with recommendation)
4. Core thesis
5. Opening hook
6. Structure outline
7. Evidence checklist (marked have / to supply)
8. Limitations

End with one line asking the next step: expand into a full draft, gather material first, or change direction.

If the user asks for only one step, do only that step. When revising a draft, start by checking it against Step 3 (core thesis), then give feedback using Step 9.

## Quality checklist
- [ ] The question is specific enough to answer in one sentence
- [ ] There is a clear gap, not a restatement of consensus
- [ ] The core thesis can work as the title
- [ ] The most valuable content is in the first 3 sentences / first 5 seconds
- [ ] Every claim has evidence or is marked "to supply"
- [ ] No invented data or cases
- [ ] Limitations are included
- [ ] The piece covers one point thoroughly

## Example
Input: "From ChatGPT to AI agents: how ordinary people can really make money with AI"

- Vague words: who are "ordinary people"; "really" compared to what; is "make money" about productivity, freelance gigs, building products, or embedding AI in an existing business
- Gap: much "make money with AI" content actually earns by selling courses; few explain what the ChatGPT-to-agent shift means for how money is made; real workflows and data are missing
- Recommended question: AI itself doesn't make money. What does AI need to be attached to in order to make money?
- Core thesis: In the ChatGPT era, value came from knowing how to ask; in the agent era, it comes from knowing how to build. The key is connecting AI to a workflow that already makes money
- Hook: Most people use AI by asking a question and getting an answer -> that saves a little time but doesn't do an extra job for you -> this piece is about turning AI into a system that works on its own, attached to a business you already have
